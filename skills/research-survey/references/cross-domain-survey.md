# Cross-Domain Literature and Mathematical Connections

Use for research inspiration, theory development, mechanism questions or a stalled
direction. Keep direct closest-work search alongside other disciplines; a distant
analogy does not replace novelty comparison in the target field.

## Search from the problem's structure

Extract the objects, relations, constraints and unexplained behavior before
selecting disciplines. For example, ask whether the problem concerns noisy state
updates, information loss, symmetry, sequential decisions or dependent samples.
These are search lenses, not assumptions already established for the project.

Translate that structure into both application terms and mathematical vocabulary.
Choose promising branches such as probability/statistics, analysis, algebra,
geometry/topology or combinatorics, and related fields such as information theory,
control or statistical physics. Use only branches with an explainable connection;
neither a fixed field count nor an exhaustive mathematics survey is required.
Keep the user's time/access limits and record which branches were not explored.

Search for definitions, theorems, empirical results, methods, proof techniques,
counterexamples and conditions of failure. Follow useful citations back to original
papers, authoritative mathematical texts or the actual source of a proof. Reviews
and terminology searches can locate leads; record which original claims were
actually inspected. An old result can be useful even when it is not new.

## Build a transfer map, not just a reading list

For each promising connection, record:

| Part | Decision-relevant content |
| --- | --- |
| Source | Identity/version, inspected theorem/section/page, access and reading scope |
| Original problem | Objects, definitions, assumptions, quantifiers and conclusion; observed result versus mathematical statement |
| Target mapping | Corresponding project objects and relations; which structures or conditions are preserved |
| Gap | Missing assumptions, changed regimes, proof obligations and possible counterexamples |
| Transfer strength | Directly applicable, requires an argument, requires a changed model, or heuristic analogy |
| Research value | Phenomenon explained, candidate insight, closest-work overlap and why this connection matters |
| Next check | Smallest discriminating derivation, source inspection or authorized exploratory check; expected effort or unknown |
| Main-line relation | Theme/thesis versions; source tool -> project object -> served claim; core or auxiliary role and bounded return condition |

Do not treat similar terminology as a structure-preserving correspondence.
For a proposed direct application, check every condition needed by the source
statement. Pay particular attention to finite versus asymptotic claims,
deterministic versus probabilistic conclusions, stationarity, dependence and
idealizations that the target does not satisfy. Missing assumptions cannot be
silently supplied by a favorable experiment.

Keep the content's role separate from evidence and proof status: an inspected
source theorem, a source-reported result, a project conjecture, a heuristic analogy
and an empirical observation support different uses. Reading a theorem statement
is not inspecting its proof. When only a snippet or abstract is available, record
the lead and precise verification needed; do not reconstruct a verified theorem
or invented bibliographic details.

Use the shared [reuse and simplification audit](../../research-navigate/references/reuse-and-simplification.md)
when a source result may replace or shorten current work. Compare the source
statement's full assumptions and quantifiers with the project content before
calling it reusable. A simpler proof, maintained implementation or standard
benchmark can be a valuable contribution asset even when it is not novel; record
the remaining project contribution rather than hiding the prior work.

## Prioritize insight and deliver

Compare likely information gain, fit to the research problem, cost and risk of
unrealistic assumptions. Prefer connections that distinguish competing explanations
or produce testable predictions over ornamental mathematical terminology. Retain
failed transfers and contradictions: they may identify a useful boundary.

Deliver the source index and evidence map with the prioritized transfer records,
candidate questions, mathematical tools worth trying, and next reading/checks.
An inaccessible source does not prevent useful modeling questions, but it limits
what may be attributed to that source. Label search coverage and unexecuted work.

Hand definitions, source locations and transfer gaps to research-ideate for new
directions, or research-design for an already selected theoretical question.
Do not restart topic selection merely because a new mathematical tool is needed.
If the user asked only for a survey, finish with this handoff rather than launching
experiments. Reuse the shared round and authorization records for continued work.
